The threshold, measured

The threshold is a sign change: below a certain physical error rate a bigger code fails less often, and above it, more often. This page shows it by simulation rather than by assertion.

What it shows

To the left of the crossing, every step up in distance lowers the failure probability, and by a larger factor the further left you go. To the right, the order reverses. That is the whole content of the word threshold. It is also why the useful question about any error-correction result is whether the logical error rate fell as the code grew.

The crossing is not one number. It moves a little with the pair of distances you compare, and it depends on the noise model and the decoder. Published values differ for that reason: Fowler and colleagues found 0.57% for their circuit-level model, and Wang and colleagues found about 0.78% for matching-based decoding with imperfect syndrome extraction. Treat {{ t.crossing }} as the answer for this model, not for a machine.

What was simulated

A rotated surface code storing one logical qubit in the Z basis, measured over as many rounds as the code distance. Every noise source has the same rate p: depolarizing noise after each gate, depolarizing noise on every data qubit before each round, bit flips after resets, and bit flips before measurements. Failures are decoded by minimum-weight perfect matching.

What it leaves out

Real devices have leakage out of the qubit states, noise that is correlated between neighbours, qubits that are worse than their neighbours, and a decoder with a deadline. A uniform noise rate is a clean way to see a threshold, and a poor description of any one machine. Nothing here predicts when a device will cross it.

Sources

  • Fowler, Mariantoni, Martinis and Cleland, Surface codes: Towards practical large-scale quantum computation, arXiv:1208.0928. A threshold of 0.57% per step for their circuit model.
  • Wang, Fowler, Stephens and Hollenberg, Threshold error rates for the toric and surface codes, arXiv:0905.0531. About 0.78% with imperfect syndrome extraction.
  • Stim, Sinter and PyMatching, the simulator, the sampling harness and the decoder used here.
